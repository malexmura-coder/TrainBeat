A practice looper. It repeats a piece of music with no gap at the seam, slower
or in another key, so a passage can be worked on until it is learned.

# What It Expects of a File

A file is assumed to be an exact loop already: the app repeats it as it is and
does not hunt for a better cut. If a file is not an exact loop you will hear
the join, and that is what Make Loop From File is for. Nothing is ever written
over your originals.

# Play File From Folder

Picks any single file with the system picker and plays it. Use this for
something you want to try once; the library is for what you come back to.

# A File Sent From Another App

Anything that can send an audio file or a score can send it here: a download, a
mail attachment, a file manager, a message. TrainBeat opens it straight in the
player, and stops whatever was playing before.

A file arriving this way is BORROWED. The permission to read it belongs to the
app that sent it and is withdrawn sooner or later - often as soon as the phone
is restarted - so the entry left in Recent Tracks will one day open on nothing.
That is why you are asked whether to keep a copy: a copy goes into the
recordings folder and is yours, and it is the copy that gets played and
remembered.

If no recordings folder has been chosen there is nowhere to put a copy, and it
says so rather than pretending. The file still plays.

# Play File From Library

The same player, over the folders you granted, with the tempo and the key
already known. Search by name, or narrow by tempo range and by key. The
half/double switch also accepts tracks at half or twice the tempo you asked
for, which is usually what you meant.

# Speed, Transpose, Train

Speed and Transpose are independent: you can slow a passage down without
lowering it, or change key at the original tempo. Train raises the speed by
its step at every repetition - set the speed low, set a small step, and the
loop walks you up to tempo on its own.

Holding a + or - button repeats it, in the trimmer.

# The Bar, and Pausing

The bar under the controls says where in the piece the loop is, and it is also
where the next Play comes in. Those are one thing, not two: drag it and press
Play to start from there, or drag it while the music runs to move there.

Which is why the button pauses rather than stops. Play carries on from the bar
you were on, not from the top - what you want when you broke off to work
something out. The same applies when the phone takes the sound away for a
message: the loop pauses and Play picks it up where it was. The Stop on the
notification is the other kind, and ends the loop outright.

Moving the cursor stops the loop for an instant and comes in again, so there
is a small gap - and with Hi-Fi on, a rendering. It is not a gesture you make
twice a bar.

On a long track that is still being decoded the bar covers the whole of it
from the start. Move ahead of what has been read and it waits for the decoder
rather than starting over; the decoder is many times faster than the music, so
the wait is short.

# Hi-Fi

Off by default, and only offered for files up to a minute.

With it off, the phone changes tempo and key while playing: instant, and good
enough for one or two semitones. With it on, the whole loop is recomputed
before playing - better sound, especially for larger shifts, at the cost of a
wait.

Above a minute it is not offered at all, because the wait and the memory both
grow with the length. To use Hi-Fi on a long track, cut a loop out of it
first.

# Play From Favourites

The star beside a name adds a track here. Same rows as the library - tempo,
key, and the key still tappable to correct it - but no search and no filters:
a shelf you keep by hand does not need filtering. The star on a row here takes
it back off the shelf.

Speed, Transpose and Train are remembered per track, starred or not, so a loop
reopens tomorrow exactly as you left it today, and the row says at what speed
you had got to.

The order is the order you starred them in. To move something to the end - the
exercise you have finished with, say - unstar it and star it again; nothing
that was remembered about it is lost.

Tempo and key come from the scan. A favourite that was never scanned - a file
opened straight from a folder, a loop just cut, a recording - has none to
show, and says so rather than inventing them.

A favourite is a pointer to a document. Move or rename the file, or withdraw
the folder, and the pointer dies: the entry stays in the list marked missing,
and tapping it forgets it.

# It Keeps Playing in the Background

A loop does not stop when the screen goes dark, when the phone locks, or when
you switch to another app to read the music. While practising you look at the
score, not at the phone.

A notification appears while it plays, and it lasts exactly as long as the
sound does. Tapping it comes back to the app, on the track that is playing and
not on the home page; the Stop on it ends the loop without opening anything,
and it works whether or not the app still has a screen of its own.

It also asks the system for the right to make noise, so a message arriving no
longer kills the loop - at worst it dips for a moment.

# Bluetooth: Speakers and Pedals

The play button on a Bluetooth speaker works, and so does a Bluetooth page-
turner pedal - the cheap kind sold for sheet music. Those send ordinary media
keys, which is all this needs. With one on the floor the loop starts and stops
without touching the phone, which is the difference between practising and
fiddling with a screen.

One button, two meanings, taken from whichever page is open: in the player it
starts and stops the loop, in Record a loop it starts and stops recording.
Bluetooth has no record command at all, so there is nothing to remember -
press the button you have.

Where a device sends next and previous as well, those step through the
favourites, in the order you starred them. So a whole session - start, stop,
on to the next exercise - runs without putting the instrument down.

Android gives the media keys to whichever app played last. If the button seems
dead, play something here once and it comes back.

# Long Tracks

A track is decoded whole into memory, because repeating a loop means having
all of it. Ten minutes of stereo is about a hundred megabytes, which a phone
will bear one at a time and not two.

So this is a looper, not a music player: it is at its best with a few seconds
to a few minutes. If a long track will not open, cut a loop out of it first
and work on that.

# Folders

Four separate things, asked once each and remembered. The library folders are
read only. The loop destination is where the trimmer writes, the recordings
folder is where the recorder writes, and the SoundFont is for MIDI.

# Scan Library

Reads every file in the granted folders once and remembers its length, tempo
and key. It can be interrupted and resumed: nothing already measured is
measured again.

Tempo and key are ESTIMATES, from the audio itself. They are usually right and
sometimes not, and a wrong key can be corrected by hand in the player with
Save Key. A MIDI file needs no guessing - tempo and key are written in the
file.

# Make Loop From File

Cuts a loop out of a longer file. The destination folder is asked when you
enter, so saving later is one tap.

There is no waveform to drag: the cursors are placed with the sliders and the
fine buttons, and you judge by ear while the loop keeps playing. The two play
buttons differ only in where they come in - one from the beginning, one from
five seconds before the end, which is where the join is and where a bad cut
gives itself away.

With Snap to Beat on, everything moves by whole beats, which is what makes a
loop come out musical. With it off, by ten milliseconds.

Auto Trim nudges both ends to the nearest quiet crossing, to remove the click
a cut in mid-waveform leaves behind. It works on anything, recordings
included: the click at the join does not care where the sound came from.

# The Drawing and the Grid

The picture follows the selection, with a little of what surrounds it, so it
stays readable however long the track is - the selected part bright, the
context dim, the two cuts marked. It answers one question quickly that the ear
answers slowly: which END the silence is at.

The grid divides the SELECTION into equal parts, not the detected tempo. If
the cut is right the peaks sit on the lines; if they drift off the lines, the
cut is wrong. The T entries add the two thinner triplet lines inside each
division.

# Record a Loop

Records from the microphone and writes a WAV. It is not a pedal looper: you
cannot hit a screen on the downbeat, so expect silence at both ends.

Stopping does not save. Listen to the take, keep it or discard it - most takes
are bad ones. Trim Silence removes the dead air at the ends and is reversible:
it stores two offsets and applies them when you save, so switching it off
restores the whole take.

A recording carries a mark, and the trimmer opens it with Snap to beat off: a
phrase played into a microphone has no measured tempo, so there is no beat
grid to trust.

# MIDI

A .mid file can be played and trimmed like audio, and it is better material:
tempo and key are read rather than estimated, and a cut on a bar line is exact
by construction - no join to find.

The Step menu matters more than it looks. Pieces open with a fill or a pickup,
so the chord sequence often starts INSIDE a bar - half way in, or a third of
the way in - and stepping by whole bars can never land there. Choose 1/2, 1/3,
1/4, 1/6 or 1/8 of a bar and it can. The thirds are for 12/8 and for anything
swung, and none of these divisions is approximate: a score counts in ticks, so
a third of a bar is an exact number of them.

But a score says which notes, not how they sound, so it needs a SoundFont. The
app ships none: choose a .sf2 yourself in Folders. Be careful which one - some
.sf2 files are effects-only supplements with no instruments in them at all,
and the app will tell you what the file you picked actually contains.

The trimmer saves either a .mid, which stays adjustable in tempo and key
forever, or a .wav, which plays anywhere without a SoundFont.
